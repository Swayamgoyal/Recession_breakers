## Member 1: Opening and foundations

### Introduction: Meera's dollar problem
- The three running characters: **Meera** (Class 12, Chennai, going to a US college), **Ravi** (her uncle in Dubai, sends remittances) and **Selvi** (garment exporter, Tiruppur).
- *Used by Member 4:* Selvi appears in the Section 8 "Real-life example" box. No new family details are added.

### 1. What is an exchange rate?
- Exchange rate as the price of one currency in terms of another; currency as "just another good with a price".
- Direct quote (₹ per $) and indirect quote ($ per ₹). The chapter always quotes **rupees per US dollar**, so a rise in the number means the rupee has weakened.
- Why the US dollar is the reference currency for Indian trade.
- *Used by Member 4:* every figure and table uses ₹/$. Figure M4-1 relies on the reader knowing that "higher means weaker rupee".

### 2. The foreign exchange market: who needs dollars and who supplies them?
- **Demand for dollars:** importers (crude oil, electronics, gold), Indians studying or travelling abroad, Indians investing overseas, repayment of foreign loans.
- **Supply of dollars:** exporters (IT services, garments, pharmaceuticals), remittances, foreign tourists, foreign investment into India.
- Who trades: banks, authorised dealers, large firms; the RBI as regulator and occasional participant.
- Terms defined there: foreign exchange market, remittances, **FDI**, **FPI**.
- *Used by Member 4:* Section 7 says "portfolio investors" and "foreign investors" without defining FDI or FPI again.

### A. The rupee's account book: the balance of payments
- Balance of payments as India's account book; current account vs capital account.
- **Current account deficit (CAD):** India spends more foreign currency than it earns from trade, which creates net demand for dollars.
- India's pattern: a large goods-trade deficit, partly offset by IT services and remittances.
- *Used by Member 4:* "current account deficit" is used in Section 7 (Table M4-1), Section D and Table M4-2 but **not defined there**. Cross-reference: [see: The rupee's account book: the balance of payments].

## Member 2: The core model

### 3. How the exchange rate is determined: demand and supply
- The market for US dollars: ₹/$ on the vertical axis, quantity of dollars on the horizontal axis.
- Why demand slopes down and supply slopes up.
- Equilibrium exchange rate; excess demand and excess supply push the rate back to equilibrium.
- **Shift of a curve vs movement along a curve.** The two worked shifts are an oil price jump (demand shifts right) and a surge of foreign investment (supply shifts right).
- *Used by Member 4:* Figure M4-2 (oil shock; US interest rate rise) and Table M4-1 depend entirely on this model. Figure M4-2 will be redrawn in Member 2's figure template.

### 4. Appreciation and depreciation: who wins, who loses?
- Appreciation and depreciation under a flexible rate; the classic confusion cleared up (₹82 → ₹86 means the rupee has *depreciated*).
- Devaluation and revaluation as the fixed-rate counterparts.
- Winners and losers table: exporters, importers, students abroad, families receiving remittances, tourists, firms with dollar loans.
- *Used by Member 4:* Section 8 opens by referring back to this table and then follows the effects through the whole economy. It does not repeat the table.

### B. Exchange rates in everyday life: buying rates, selling rates and currency risk
- Buying rate, selling rate and spread; card markups.
- **Currency risk** through Selvi's three-month dollar contract; forward contracts and hedging.
- *Used by Member 4:* the Selvi box in Section 8 deliberately does **not** repeat hedging. It looks at a different side of the same business: imported inputs and the buyer asking for a lower price.

## Member 3: Systems and policy

### 5. Exchange rate systems: fixed, flexible and managed
- Gold standard and Bretton Woods; fixed (pegged), flexible (floating) and managed float.
- India's journey: link to sterling, basket peg, **the 1991 devaluation**, LERMS (1992), market-determined rate (1993).
- India today as a managed float.
- **Case study box: the 1991 balance-of-payments crisis.** Member 3 owns these facts.
- *Used by Member 4:* Section D compares Sri Lanka and Pakistan with 1991 but refers to Member 3's box instead of retelling it. The "Reading the rupee's history" box hints at this section, because band 1 (1991) was an official devaluation, not a market move. The India 1991 column of Table M4-2 must match Member 3's figures.

### C. The RBI and India's foreign exchange reserves
- What reserves are (foreign currency assets, gold, SDRs, IMF reserve position) and why a country holds them.
- **RBI intervention:** selling dollars from reserves adds to supply and slows a fall in the rupee.
- **Import cover;** the limits of intervention.
- *Used by Member 4:* Section D opens from here ("reserves work like a family's savings for a bad year") and asks what happens when they run out. "Import cover" and "intervention" are used without being defined again.

### 6. Nominal and real exchange rates, and purchasing power parity
- Nominal vs real exchange rate; PPP and why it does not hold exactly.
- If Indian prices rise faster than American prices, Indian goods become less competitive.
- *Used by Member 4:* Section 7 uses this to explain the slow, long-run climb in Figure M4-1 (inflation differences).
