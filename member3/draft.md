# Exchange Rate Systems

We learnt about the exchange rates, how they influence our life, what makes Indian Rupee appreciate or depreciate, but how actually are the exchange rates determined and what exchange systems have been used in the history? To answer this we first need to understand the different types of exchange rate systems.

### Types of Exchange Rate Systems

An exchange rate system is how a country governs the valuation of its currency relative to others. They are primarily of three categories:

1. Fixed Exchange Rate System: In a fixed (or pegged) system, a country’s monetary authority sets and upholds a constant value of its currency against different commodities or currencies such as against a single currency (against U.S. dollar), a basket of currencies with different weights or importance given to the currencies inside it, or against commodities like gold. Central banks, such as RBI in India, intervene in the foreign exchange market by buying or selling foreign currency so that the same rate of the currency can be maintained. The main advantage of this system is that it provides certainty and stability, which is especially helpful for businesses that trade across borders, since they know exactly what exchange rate to expect. It also helps keep inflation in check. However, the downside is that it requires the country to hold large reserves of foreign currency to defend the fixed rate, and if the rate is set incorrectly, it can lead to economic problems like the 1991 crisis India faced.
2. Floating Exchange Rate System: In the floating system, the value of the currency is determined purely by the market forces of supply and demand, with little to no direct government control. These systems are considered self-correcting as the exchange rate adjustments rectify the trade imbalances over time. The advantage here is that the government does not need to maintain large foreign reserves, and the currency adjusts automatically to reflect the true state of the economy. The disadvantage is that exchange rates can be very unpredictable and volatile, making it difficult for businesses to plan ahead and potentially causing economic instability.
3. Managed Floating (Dirty Float) Exchange Rate System: This is a hybrid model of the other two systems, where the currency values are primarily set by the market supply and demand, but occasionally, the central bank steps in to influence or stabilize the rate to prevent excessive volatility, due to some policy goals, etc. The benefit of this system is that it enjoys the flexibility of floating rates while the central bank can step in during times of extreme volatility to prevent harm to the economy. The drawback is that it can be unpredictable, since markets may not always know when or why the central bank will intervene, and there is a risk that governments may misuse interventions to artificially weaken their currency for trade advantages.

Now that we know about the different categories of the exchange rate systems, let’s see the history of the exchange rate systems used in India.

### History of Indian Exchange Rate Systems

#### 1947-1971

Following the Independence of India in 1947, the country employed a fixed exchange rate regime by adopting the par value mechanism under the Bretton Woods Agreement, established in 1944. Under the Bretton Woods system, U.S. dollar was based on gold, and the other currencies were pegged to the value of the U.S. dollar’s value. The U.S. government committed to buy and sell gold at a fixed rate of $35 per ounce, and the other countries participating in it agreed to keep their currencies at a fixed peg against the U.S. dollar with only diversions of 1% allowed. India adopted a similar system. India fixed the rupee’s par value in terms of gold, using the British Pound Sterling as its intervention currency, instead of USD, largely due to its economic and colonial ties with Britain.

#### 1971-1975

In 1971, there was a dramatic shift called the Nixon Shock. The U.S. ended their commitment of converting USD to gold. This move led to major global currencies adopting the floating exchange rate systems. India had shifted its peg to USD in August 1971 but later reverted to British Pound Sterling in December of the same year. This shift was advantageous to them as they could depreciate the rupee in line with global inflation and commodity price hikes, and hence also allowed them to show relatively more resilience to the global oil crisis during this time. The automatic depreciation due to depreciation of sterling improved India’s export competitiveness.

#### 1975-1991

After the British Pound Sterling itself became increasingly unstable during the mid-1970s, India moved away from pegging to a single currency and adopted a basket-linked exchange rate system in 1975. Under this system, the value of the Indian Rupee was linked to a weighted basket of currencies such as the U.S. Dollar, British Pound, Yen, and Franc. The Reserve Bank of India decided the composition of this basket and the weight given to each currency in it. This system made the Rupee more stable because even if one currency in the basket became weak, the others could balance it out. The RBI still had full control over the exchange rate and adjusted it from time to time based on economic conditions, keeping the system firmly in the fixed exchange rate category.

However, by the late 1980s, India was spending far more on imports than it was earning from exports, and the government was borrowing heavily from abroad to cover this gap. This led to a dangerous build-up of foreign debt. The situation became a full-blown crisis by 1991.

#### 1991: The Balance of Payments Crisis

In 1991, India faced its worst economic crisis after Independence. The country’s foreign exchange reserves had fallen so critically low that India could barely pay for two weeks of imports. \DidYouKnow (To avoid defaulting on its international payments, the government had to physically airlift 67 tonnes of gold to the Bank of England and the Bank of Japan as collateral to secure emergency loans, a deeply humbling moment for the nation.)

To restore confidence and correct the trade imbalance, the government devalued the Rupee sharply in two steps over just three days (July 1 to 3, 1991), bringing its value down by around 18 to 20% against the U.S. Dollar. A cheaper Rupee made Indian exports more competitive globally and made imports more expensive, helping to reduce the trade deficit.

#### 1992-1993: Dual Exchange Rate System (LERMS)

As a transitional step toward a more open exchange rate system, India introduced the Liberalised Exchange Rate Management System (LERMS) in March 1992. Under this system, India operated with two exchange rates simultaneously: an official rate set by the RBI, and a market rate determined by supply and demand. Exporters were required to surrender 40% of their foreign exchange earnings to the RBI at the lower official rate, while the remaining 60% could be converted at the higher market rate. Importers and others needing foreign exchange had to buy it at the market rate. This dual-rate arrangement was designed to gradually expose India’s currency to market forces without letting go of all government control at once, making it a carefully managed transition.

#### 1993-Present: Market-Determined Exchange Rate (Managed Float)

In March 1993, India took the decisive step of unifying the two exchange rates into a single, market-determined exchange rate. The dual-rate system was abolished, and the value of the Rupee was now primarily set by the forces of supply and demand in the foreign exchange market. This effectively made India’s exchange rate system a managed floating system, the same hybrid model described earlier in this chapter.

Under this system, which continues to this day, the RBI does not fix the Rupee’s value, but it does intervene in the market from time to time by buying or selling foreign exchange to prevent excessive or abrupt fluctuations in the exchange rate. In 1994, India also made the Rupee fully convertible on the current account, meaning individuals and businesses could freely exchange Rupees for foreign currencies for trade in goods, services, and income, without needing prior government approval. However, full convertibility on the capital account (for investments and borrowings) is still not in place, and the RBI carefully regulates those flows to maintain financial stability.

## The RBI and India's Foreign Exchange Reserves

We have seen how the RBI intervenes in the foreign exchange market to stabilise the Rupee. But what exactly does it intervene with? The answer lies in India's foreign exchange reserves, a stockpile of assets that the RBI holds and manages on behalf of the country.

India's foreign exchange reserves are made up of four components. The largest part is foreign currency assets, which are holdings of major currencies like the U.S. Dollar, Euro, and Pound Sterling, typically invested in safe government securities abroad. The second component is gold, which India holds as a universally accepted store of value. The third is Special Drawing Rights (SDR), an international reserve asset created by the International Monetary Fund (IMF) that member countries can use to settle payments with other member nations. The fourth is India's reserve position with the IMF, which is the amount India can draw from the IMF on demand without conditions.

Why does a country need to hold these reserves at all? There are three main reasons. First, reserves act as a buffer against economic shocks. If a sudden crisis hits, such as a sharp fall in exports or a global financial panic, the country can draw on its reserves to manage the impact. Second, reserves ensure that the country can pay for its imports even during difficult times. A commonly used measure for this is import cover, which tells us how many months of imports a country could pay for using only its reserves, with no additional income. A higher import cover signals a stronger safety net. Third, large reserves send a positive signal to foreign investors, showing that the country is financially stable and can meet its obligations, which encourages investment.

Now let us look at how the RBI actually uses these reserves to manage the Rupee's value. Suppose the Rupee is falling too fast against the U.S. Dollar, perhaps because too many people are selling Rupees to buy Dollars. The RBI steps in by selling Dollars from its reserves into the foreign exchange market. This increases the supply of Dollars, making the Dollar relatively cheaper and the Rupee relatively more valuable, slowing or reversing the Rupee's fall. The opposite happens when the Rupee is rising too fast: the RBI buys Dollars, increasing demand for the Dollar and gently pushing the Rupee's value back down. Through these buy and sell operations, the RBI smooths out sharp swings without trying to fix the exchange rate at a specific level.

However, this tool has clear limits. Foreign exchange reserves, however large, are finite. If strong and sustained market pressure is pushing the Rupee in one direction, the RBI cannot fight it indefinitely without draining its reserves. This is precisely what happened in 1991, when India's reserves ran dangerously low and the government could no longer defend the Rupee's value. It is a reminder that while reserves give the RBI significant power to stabilise the currency in the short run, they cannot override fundamental economic forces over the long run.

## Nominal and Real Exchange Rates, and Purchasing Power Parity

So far, we have talked about the exchange rate as a single number: how many Rupees it takes to buy one U.S. Dollar. This is called the nominal exchange rate, and it is what you see on a currency exchange board or a news ticker. However, this number alone does not tell the full story of a currency's strength.

Consider this: suppose the nominal exchange rate stays exactly the same, say, 83 Rupees per Dollar, for an entire year. At first glance, nothing has changed. But if prices in India rise by 10% over that year while prices in the U.S. stay flat, Indian goods have become 10% more expensive for American buyers, even though the exchange rate did not move. India has effectively become less competitive as an exporter, not because of any change in the exchange rate, but because of inflation at home. This is what the real exchange rate captures. It adjusts the nominal rate for differences in price levels between two countries. If Indian prices are rising faster than American prices, the real exchange rate is appreciating even when the nominal rate is unchanged, making Indian exports relatively more expensive.

This idea connects directly to a concept called Purchasing Power Parity, or PPP. PPP is based on a simple idea: a basket of goods should cost the same in every country, once prices are converted into the same currency. If a basket of groceries costs 500 Rupees in India and $6 in the U.S., then the PPP exchange rate would be roughly 83 Rupees per Dollar. If the actual exchange rate is very different from this, it suggests one currency is overvalued or undervalued.

However, PPP does not hold exactly in the real world, for a few reasons. First, transport costs and import taxes mean that identical goods are not equally priced across borders. Second, and more importantly, many goods and services simply cannot be traded across countries at all. A haircut, a local bus ride, or a meal at a street stall in India costs far less than the equivalent in the U.S., not because of currency misalignment, but because these services are priced according to local wages and cannot be exported to equalise prices.

Despite its limitations, PPP is extremely useful for comparing living standards across countries. If you use the nominal exchange rate to compare incomes, a country with low prices appears poorer than it actually is. PPP adjustments correct for this by accounting for what money can actually buy in each country, giving a fairer picture of real living standards.
