# Introduction: Meera’s dollar problem

In October 2026, Meera, a Class 12 student in Chennai, won a place at a college in the United States. Its first-year fees were US\$40,000, the same as a year earlier. Yet in rupees they now cost about ₹2.5 lakh more. The college had not raised its price. The price of the dollar had gone up.

::: {custom-style="Key Term"}
The fees are hypothetical. The rates are real closing rates: ₹88.67 per US\$ on 13 October 2025^1^ and ₹95.05 on 15 October 2026.^2^ 40,000 × 6.38 = ₹2,55,200.
:::

Her uncle Ravi, an electrician in Dubai, saw the other side. The money he sends his parents in Thanjavur every month now arrives as more rupees. One price, two opposite outcomes. This chapter explains what that price is, who sets it and why it keeps moving.

# 1 What is an exchange rate?

Rupees buy chai in Chennai, but coffee in New York needs dollars and a meal in Dubai needs dirhams. Whenever goods, services or savings cross a border, one currency must be swapped for another. The price of that swap is the **exchange rate**: the price of one currency in terms of another.^3^ In India it is set mainly by buyers and sellers in a market, so it changes minute by minute.

Indian banks use the **direct quote**, the number of rupees needed to buy one unit of foreign currency, as in US\$1 = ₹95.^4^ The **indirect quote** flips this to ₹1 = US\$0.0105. This chapter always uses the direct quote (₹/\$), so a bigger number means a weaker rupee.

The rupee is watched mostly against the US dollar, the world’s working currency, which was on one side of 89 per cent of all currency trades in April 2025.^6^ Crude oil, India’s biggest import, is priced in dollars, and only about 6 per cent of India’s exports are invoiced in rupees,^7^ even though the Reserve Bank of India (RBI) has allowed trade to be settled in rupees since July 2022.^8^ The dollar even reaches the UAE dirham, which has been fixed, or **pegged**, at 3.6725 per US dollar since 1997^9^ (see Section 5). So when a dollar costs more rupees, so does every dirham Ravi sends home.

# 2 The foreign exchange market: who needs dollars and who supplies them?

Currencies are traded in the **foreign exchange (forex) market**, a worldwide electronic network of banks, dealers and large firms with no building and no closing bell.^10^

Anyone who must pay abroad needs dollars: importers, students like Meera, travellers, Indians investing abroad and borrowers repaying foreign loans. They make up the **demand for foreign exchange**. Anyone who earns from abroad brings dollars in: exporters like Selvi, whose garment unit in Tiruppur sells T-shirts to American shops, foreign tourists, foreign investors and workers like Ravi. Their **remittances** made India the world’s largest recipient in 2025, at US\$150.7 billion.^11^ Together they make up the **supply of foreign exchange**.^3^ Each deal is two purchases at once, because Meera’s father buys dollars by selling rupees.^10^ The exchange rate is the price at which the two sides match (Figure 2, see Section 4).

Yet most forex trading is not about trade. About US\$9.6 trillion changed hands every day in April 2025,^6^ so the market turns over a year of world exports (US\$33 trillion in 2024^12^) in under four days. Most of it is investors moving savings. **Foreign direct investment (FDI)** buys a lasting stake of 10 per cent or more in a business, such as a car factory near Chennai, and stays for years. **Foreign portfolio investment (FPI)** buys shares and bonds and can leave in a day, so it can jolt the rupee^10^ (see Section 8).

Under the Foreign Exchange Management Act, 1999, only dealers licensed by the RBI, such as banks (**authorised dealers**) and money changers, may deal in foreign currency. The RBI sets the rules and at times buys or sells dollars itself (see Section 6).

::: {custom-style="Box Navy"}
In October, Selvi sells T-shirts worth US\$20,000 to a New York shop that will pay in January. At today’s **spot rate**, the rate for a deal settled now, of ₹95, that is ₹19 lakh. If the rupee strengthens to ₹90 by January, she gets only ₹18 lakh and her profit could vanish. So she signs a **forward contract** with her bank, which fixes today a **forward rate** at which the bank will buy her dollars in January. Importers use the same tool against a weaker rupee.

# 3 The rupee’s account book: the balance of payments

Every dollar that enters or leaves India is recorded in the **balance of payments (BoP)**, the record of all economic transactions between a country’s residents and the rest of the world over a period such as a year.^3^ The **current account** records trade in goods and services, income and transfers such as remittances. The **capital account** records the buying and selling of assets, such as FDI, FPI and foreign loans. When a country spends more foreign currency on its current account than it earns, it runs a **current account deficit (CAD)**, which must be paid for by net capital inflows or by drawing down reserves.^3^

India imports far more goods than it exports, mainly oil, gold and electronics. But **invisibles**, which are services such as software, income and transfers such as remittances, brought in a net US\$312 billion in 2025-26. That left a CAD of only US\$25.2 billion, about 0.6 per cent of GDP.^13^ Capital flows did not cover even this, because foreign portfolio investors took out a net US\$16.4 billion. So the RBI drew about US\$23.6 billion from its reserves (see Section 6).

# 4 How the exchange rate is determined: demand and supply

## Finding the equilibrium exchange rate

People and businesses demand US dollars to make payments abroad, while dollars enter India’s foreign exchange market through exports, remittances and inward investment. A demand-and-supply model shows how these forces set the exchange rate.

The demand curve slopes downward. When a dollar costs fewer rupees, foreign goods, foreign education and travel abroad become cheaper for Indians, so they wish to buy more dollars. The supply curve slopes upward. When each dollar fetches more rupees, selling abroad becomes more rewarding for exporters and sending money home becomes more attractive for workers abroad, so more dollars are offered.

The two curves meet at the **equilibrium exchange rate**, where quantities demanded and supplied are equal and the market is in balance. Figure 3(a) shows this intersection.

![](figures/docx/ds_basic.png){width=100%}

::: {custom-style="Caption Label"}
**Figure 3.** How changes in demand and supply affect the exchange rate.
:::

::: {custom-style="Caption Note"}
(a) Demand and supply meet at E, ₹82 per US dollar. (b) Demand shifts right from D~0~ to D~1~. The new equilibrium E~1~ is at ₹86, so the rupee has depreciated. (c) Supply shifts right from S~0~ to S~1~. The new equilibrium is at ₹79, so the rupee has appreciated.
:::

## What happens when demand or supply changes?

When demand or supply changes, the curves shift and the market moves to a new equilibrium.

**An increase in the demand for dollars.** Suppose more Indians begin travelling to the United States or purchasing foreign goods and services. At each exchange rate, buyers now wish to purchase more dollars than before, so the demand curve shifts to the right. In panel (b) of Figure 3, the equilibrium moves from ₹82 to ₹86 per US dollar. Buyers must now pay more rupees for each dollar, which means that the rupee has depreciated against the dollar.

**An increase in the supply of dollars.** Now suppose Indian exporters receive more dollar payments from customers abroad. At each exchange rate, sellers are willing to supply more dollars than before, so the supply curve shifts to the right. In panel (c), the exchange rate falls from ₹82 to ₹79. Each dollar can now be exchanged for fewer rupees, so the rupee has appreciated against the dollar.

In reality, demand and supply often shift together—stronger import demand raises the demand for dollars while a surge in export earnings raises their supply. The resulting exchange rate depends on both shifts, which is why we must consider both curves to find the new equilibrium (see Section 8).

# 5 Exchange rate systems: fixed, flexible and managed

We have learnt what exchange rates are, how they affect our lives, and what makes the rupee appreciate or depreciate. But does the market always set the rate, and what systems has India used in its history? To answer this we first need to understand the different types of exchange rate systems.

## Types of exchange rate systems

An **exchange rate system** is the set of rules a country follows to manage the value of its currency against other currencies. There are three main types:

1. **Fixed exchange rate system.** In a **fixed (pegged) exchange rate** system, the central bank holds the rate at a set value against another currency, a basket of currencies or gold. It buys or sells foreign currency to keep the rate steady.
2. **Floating exchange rate system.** In a **floating (flexible) exchange rate** system, the rate is set by demand and supply in the market, with little government control. This is the market described in Section 4.
3. **Managed floating (dirty float) system.** A **managed float** is a mix of the two. The rate is mainly set by the market, with the central bank stepping in to prevent sharp swings.

Each system has its supporters, and each comes with risks, as Table 1 shows.

::: {custom-style="Caption Label"}
**Table 1.** Fixed, floating or managed: pros and cons.
:::

| **System** | **Pros** | **Cons** |
|-------------|------------------------|------------------------|
| Fixed | Stable, predictable rate. Keeps inflation in check. | Needs large reserves. A wrong rate can cause a crisis (India, 1991). |
| Floating | Self-correcting. No large reserves needed. | Volatile and hard to plan around. |
| Managed float | Flexibility with a safety net. | Interventions can be unpredictable, or used to weaken the currency. |

## India’s exchange rate system today

Since March 1993, India has operated under a market-determined exchange rate, known as a managed float. The dual exchange rate system was abolished, and the value of the rupee became primarily set by the forces of supply and demand. Under this system, the RBI does not fix the rupee’s value, but it does intervene from time to time by buying or selling foreign exchange to prevent excessive or abrupt fluctuations.

# 6 The RBI and India’s foreign exchange reserves

We have seen how the RBI intervenes in the foreign exchange market to stabilise the rupee. But what exactly does it intervene with? The answer lies in India’s **foreign exchange reserves**, a stockpile of assets that the RBI holds and manages on behalf of the country.

India’s foreign exchange reserves are made up of four components. The largest part is **foreign currency assets**, holdings of major currencies like the US dollar, euro and pound sterling, typically invested in safe foreign government securities. The second component is **gold**. The third is **Special Drawing Rights (SDR)**, an international reserve asset created by the IMF that member countries can use to settle payments with other nations. The fourth is India’s **reserve position with the IMF**, the amount India can draw on demand without conditions.

Why does a country hold these reserves? There are three main reasons. First, they act as a buffer against economic shocks: if exports collapse or a financial panic hits, reserves can cushion the blow. Second, they ensure the country can pay for its imports even during difficult times. A commonly used measure is **import cover**, which tells us how many months of imports a country could pay for using only its reserves. Third, large reserves signal financial stability to foreign investors, encouraging investment.

Figure 4 shows how India’s foreign exchange reserves have grown since 1990.

![](figures/reserves.png){width=100%}

::: {custom-style="Caption Label"}
**Figure 4.** India’s foreign exchange reserves.
:::

Now let us look at how the RBI uses these reserves to manage the rupee’s value. Suppose the rupee is falling too fast against the US dollar, perhaps because too many people are selling rupees to buy dollars. The RBI steps in by selling dollars from its reserves into the foreign exchange market. This **intervention** increases the supply of dollars, making the dollar relatively cheaper and the rupee relatively more valuable, slowing or reversing the rupee’s fall. The opposite happens when the rupee is rising too fast: the RBI buys dollars, increasing demand for the dollar and gently pushing the rupee’s value back down.

However, this tool has clear limits. Foreign exchange reserves, however large, are finite. If strong and sustained market pressure is pushing the rupee in one direction, the RBI cannot fight it indefinitely. This is precisely what happened in 1991, and it is a reminder that while reserves give the RBI significant power to stabilise the currency in the short run, they cannot override fundamental economic forces over the long run (see Section 10).

# 7 Nominal and real exchange rates, and purchasing power parity

So far, we have talked about the exchange rate as a single number: how many rupees it takes to buy one US dollar. This is called the **nominal exchange rate**, and it is what you see on a currency exchange board or a news ticker. However, this number alone does not tell the full story of a currency’s strength.

Consider this: suppose the nominal exchange rate stays exactly the same, say ₹83 per US dollar, for an entire year. At first glance, nothing has changed. But if prices in India rise by 10 per cent over that year while prices in the US stay flat, Indian goods have become 10 per cent more expensive for American buyers, even though the exchange rate did not move. India has effectively become less competitive as an exporter, not because of any change in the exchange rate, but because of inflation at home. This is what the **real exchange rate** captures. It adjusts the nominal rate for differences in price levels between two countries. If Indian prices are rising faster than American prices, the real exchange rate is appreciating even when the nominal rate is unchanged, making Indian exports relatively more expensive.

This idea connects directly to a concept called **purchasing power parity (PPP)**. PPP is based on a simple idea: a basket of goods should cost the same in every country, once prices are converted into the same currency. If a basket of groceries costs ₹500 in India and \$6 in the US, then the PPP exchange rate would be roughly ₹83 per US dollar. If the actual exchange rate is very different from this, it suggests one currency is overvalued or undervalued.

However, PPP does not hold exactly in the real world. First, transport costs and import taxes mean that identical goods are not equally priced across borders. Second, many goods and services simply cannot be traded across countries at all. A haircut, a local bus ride, or a meal at a street stall in India costs far less than the equivalent in the US, not because of currency misalignment, but because these services are priced according to local wages and cannot be exported to equalise prices.

Despite its limitations, PPP is extremely useful for comparing living standards across countries. If you use the nominal exchange rate to compare incomes, a country with low prices appears poorer than it actually is. PPP adjustments correct for this by accounting for what money can actually buy in each country, giving a fairer picture of real living standards.

::: {custom-style="Box Teal Heading"}
**Did you know?** The Maharaja Mac index
:::

::: {custom-style="Box Teal"}
Every year, *The Economist* magazine publishes the Big Mac index, which compares the price of a McDonald’s burger across countries to estimate whether currencies are overvalued or undervalued. Since beef is not widely eaten in India, McDonald’s India sells a Maharaja Mac instead, and *The Economist* uses its chicken version as India’s entry in the index.

In January 2023, a Maharaja Mac cost ₹207 in India and a Big Mac cost \$5.36 in the US. This implies a PPP exchange rate of about ₹38.6 per US dollar, while the actual rate was around ₹81.7. This suggests the rupee was undervalued by about 53 per cent against the dollar, meaning money goes much further in India than the nominal exchange rate would suggest.^14^
:::

::: {custom-style="Box Teal Heading"}
**Did you know?** PPP and India’s income
:::

::: {custom-style="Box Teal"}
India’s nominal gross national income (GNI) per person, converted at market exchange rates, was about \$2,760 in 2025. Adjusted for PPP, it was about \$11,600, roughly 4.2 times higher.^15^ This gap exists because everyday goods and services in India are far cheaper than in high-income countries, so the same amount of money buys much more. International organisations like the World Bank and IMF prefer PPP figures when comparing living standards across countries for exactly this reason.
:::

# 8 What moves the rupee?

Behind every headline such as ‘Rupee falls 30 paise against the dollar’ sits the model from Section 4. Whenever the rupee moves, ask one question: did something change the demand for dollars, the supply of dollars, or both?

Figure 5 traces the rupee since 1991. The line climbs from about ₹18 per US dollar to about ₹95, a level first crossed in 2026.^16^ Long, quiet stretches are broken by sudden jumps.

The slow part of the climb is mainly about inflation. Prices in India have usually risen faster than in the United States, so Indian goods slowly become dearer for foreign buyers (see Section 7). Fewer dollars come in, more go out, and the rupee drifts down year after year.

The sudden jumps are usually about money crossing borders. When foreigners buy Indian shares, bonds or businesses, dollars flow in, and when they sell, dollars flow out. These **capital flows** drive most short-run swings, because portfolio money can leave quickly (see Section 2).

What makes this money move? Very often, interest rates. Investors compare what they can earn on an American government bond with what they can earn on an Indian one. The gap between the two is the **interest rate differential**. When the US Federal Reserve raises its interest rate, the gap narrows. Foreign investors sell Indian assets and take their dollars home, and the supply of dollars shifts to the left (Figure 6b).

Crude oil is the other big mover. India imports about 88 per cent of the crude oil it uses.^18^ When the world price of oil rises, refiners need more dollars to buy the same number of barrels, and the demand for dollars shifts to the right (Figure 6a). A widening current account deficit (see Section 3) has a similar effect.

![](figures/docx/ds_shocks.png){width=100%}

::: {custom-style="Caption Label"}
**Figure 6.** Two shocks in the market for US dollars (hypothetical numbers).
:::

::: {custom-style="Caption Note"}
(a) Dearer oil means refiners want more dollars at every rate, so demand shifts right to D~1~. Buyers bid the rate up from ₹85 to ₹90 at E~1~. Each dollar now costs more rupees, which is exactly what a depreciation of the rupee means.
:::

::: {custom-style="Caption Note"}
(b) Foreign investors move funds to the United States, so supply shifts left to S~1~. The rate rises from ₹85 to ₹89, and fewer dollars change hands.

Markets also look ahead. If traders expect the rupee to fall, importers rush to buy dollars today and exporters hold on to the dollars they already have. Buying or selling a currency to profit from its expected future price is called **speculation**. It can turn a fear into a fact: the rupee falls partly because so many people acted on the belief that it would.

Real episodes usually mix these forces. In the global financial crisis of 2008 and the COVID-19 panic of 2020, investors everywhere fled to the safety of the US dollar, and the rupee fell even though India caused neither crisis.

# 9 Why exchange rates matter for India

Whether a change in the rupee helps or hurts you depends on whether you earn dollars, spend them or owe them.

Start with those who earn dollars. When the rupee weakens, every dollar an exporter earns turns into more rupees. When the rupee moved from ₹90 to ₹95, the same \$20,000 order from Selvi’s American buyer brought in ₹19 lakh instead of ₹18 lakh. She can keep the extra ₹1 lakh, or cut her dollar price to win more orders. Either way, Indian goods become easier to sell, and we say that their **export competitiveness** has improved.

::: {custom-style="Key Term"}
**export competitiveness**  The ability of a country’s firms to sell goods and services abroad at prices that foreign buyers are willing to pay.
:::

Now consider those who spend dollars. India imports crude oil, gold, electronics, fertiliser and edible oil, almost all priced in dollars. A weaker rupee makes each of them dearer in rupees, even when the dollar price has not changed. Diesel runs the trucks that carry vegetables, grain and medicines, so costlier fuel spreads into the price of almost everything (Figure 7). A rise in prices that comes from dearer imports is called **imported inflation**.

![](figures/docx/imported_inflation.png){width=100%}

::: {custom-style="Caption Label"}
**Figure 7.** How a weaker rupee becomes imported inflation.
:::

::: {custom-style="Caption Note"}
Each step takes time, so the full effect on prices can take several months to appear.
:::

::: {custom-style="Box Navy Heading"}
**Worked example** The same barrel, a different price
:::

Suppose a barrel of crude oil costs \$80 on the world market.

• At ₹85 per US dollar, India pays 80 × 85 = ₹6,800 per barrel.

• At ₹90 per US dollar, India pays 80 × 90 = ₹7,200 per barrel.

The oil has not become any dearer in dollars, yet India pays ₹400 more for every barrel, a rise of about 5.9 per cent.
:::

Then there are those who owe dollars. A company that borrowed in dollars, such as an airline paying for new aircraft, must now find more rupees to repay the same loan. A sharp fall in the rupee can turn a healthy balance sheet into a worrying one.

This matters to the RBI. When it raises its policy interest rate (the repo rate), foreign money tends to flow in and the rupee strengthens. Imports become cheaper and inflation cools, although exporters find life harder. This route from interest rates to spending and prices, working through the exchange rate, is called the **exchange rate channel** of monetary policy.^4^

# 10 When currencies collapse: crises in South Asia

Foreign exchange reserves work like a family’s savings for a bad year (see Section 6). A country that runs out of dollars to pay for its imports and foreign loans can no longer protect its currency. The result is a **currency crisis**: a sudden, steep fall in the value of a currency, usually together with shortages and soaring prices. Two of India’s neighbours have lived through one recently.

**Sri Lanka, 2022.**  Large tax cuts in 2019 reduced the government’s income. The COVID-19 pandemic then emptied the tourist hotels that earned much of the country’s foreign currency, while big foreign loan repayments kept falling due. The central bank spent its reserves holding the Sri Lankan rupee at about 200 per dollar, until by March 2022 only about US\$1.9 billion was left.^19^

Once the currency was allowed to float in March 2022, it crashed to about 356 per dollar by May (Figure 8). Fuel, cooking gas and medicines ran short, and people queued for hours outside petrol pumps. In April 2022 the government stopped paying its foreign debts, Sri Lanka’s first **sovereign default**. Inflation reached 69.8 per cent in September 2022.^20^ In March 2023 the IMF approved a loan of about US\$3 billion,^21^ and prices slowly began to settle.

**Pakistan, 2022–23.**  Pakistan came close to the same edge. High oil prices and the 2022 floods widened its current account deficit, and talks with the IMF stalled. By February 2023 its reserves had fallen to about US\$2.9 billion, barely enough for a few weeks of imports.^23^ The Pakistani rupee slid to a record low of about 307 per dollar in September 2023.^24^ A US\$3 billion **IMF programme**, approved in July 2023, helped Pakistan avoid default.^25^

::: {custom-style="Key Term"}
**IMF programme**  A loan from the International Monetary Fund to a country short of foreign currency, paid in instalments on condition that the country carries out agreed policy changes.
:::

**What about India?**  Both stories echo India’s own crisis of 1991 (see Section 5), when reserves covered only about two weeks of imports. India’s position today is much stronger. Its reserves stood at about US\$682 billion in May 2026, enough for about 11 months of imports.^26^ Under a managed float, the rupee adjusts a little every day, so pressure is released gradually instead of building up behind a fixed rate.

The lesson from all three cases is that currencies rarely collapse because of a single bad event. Low reserves, heavy foreign-currency debt and a large current account deficit are like dry wood. A pandemic, an oil shock or a flood only supplies the spark.

::: {custom-style="Box Orange Heading"}
**When economists disagree** Is the IMF’s medicine too bitter?
:::

::: {custom-style="Box Orange"}
IMF loans come with conditions. Under its 2023 programme, for example, Sri Lanka agreed to raise taxes and to price fuel and electricity so that they cover their costs.

Supporters argue that the conditions fix the problems that caused the crisis and reassure other lenders. Without reform, they say, the loan would simply be spent and the crisis would return.

Critics reply that raising taxes and cutting spending in the middle of a crisis makes the slump deeper, and the burden falls hardest on poor families, who spend most of their income on food and fuel.

What do you think? Should help in a crisis come with strings attached?
