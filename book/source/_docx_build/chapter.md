# Introduction: Meera’s dollar problem

In October 2026, Meera, a Class 12 student in Chennai, got the email her family had been waiting for: a college in the United States had offered her a place. The first-year fees were US\$40,000, exactly what the college had charged a year earlier. Yet when her father asked his bank what the fees would cost in rupees, the answer was about ₹2.5 lakh more than it would have been a year before. The college had not raised its price. The price of the dollar had gone up.

::: {custom-style="Key Term"}
The fees are hypothetical. The exchange rates are real closing rates: ₹88.67 per US dollar on 13 October 2025^1^ and ₹95.05 on 15 October 2026.^2^ 40,000 × (95.05 − 88.67) = ₹2,55,200.
:::

Meera’s uncle Ravi, an electrician in Dubai, had the opposite surprise. Every month he sends part of his salary home to his parents in Thanjavur. This year the same amount arrived as more rupees than before.

One family, one price, and two very different outcomes. This chapter explains what that price is, who sets it, and why it keeps moving.

# 1 What is an exchange rate?

Chai in Chennai is paid for in rupees, but coffee in New York needs US dollars and a meal in Dubai needs dirhams. So whenever goods, services or savings cross a border, one currency has to be swapped for another. The price of that swap is the **exchange rate**: the price of one currency in terms of another.^3^ In India it is set mainly by buyers and sellers in a market, and like the price of onions it can change from minute to minute.

Indian banks write the rate as a **direct quote**, “US\$1 = ₹95”, which is the number of rupees needed to buy one dollar.^4^ Flip it and you get the **indirect quote**, “₹1 = US\$0.0105”, which is the number of dollars one rupee buys. This chapter always uses the direct quote, rupees per dollar (₹/\$), so a bigger number means a weaker rupee. At ₹95 instead of ₹88, every rupee buys fewer dollars (see Section 5).

## Why everyone watches the dollar

Why does the news talk only about the rupee’s price in dollars? Because the dollar is the world’s working currency. In April 2025 it was on one side of 89 per cent of all currency trades.^6^ Crude oil, India’s biggest import, is priced in dollars, and only about 6 per cent of India’s exports are invoiced in rupees.^7^ Since July 2022 the Reserve Bank of India (RBI) has allowed Indian firms to settle payments for their imports and exports in rupees,^8^ but most still choose the dollar, because almost everyone, everywhere, will accept it.

The dollar even reaches currencies that seem unrelated. The UAE dirham, which Ravi earns, has been fixed at 3.6725 dirhams per US dollar since 1997.^9^ Tying a currency to another at a set rate like this is called a **peg** (see Section 6). So when a dollar costs more rupees, so does a dirham, and every dirham Ravi sends home turns into more rupees.

# 2 The foreign exchange market: who needs dollars and who supplies them?

Currencies are bought and sold in the **foreign exchange market**, or forex market. It has no building and no closing bell. It is a worldwide network of banks, dealers and large firms that trade with each other electronically, and as the day moves from Tokyo and Singapore to Mumbai, London and New York, the trading moves with it.^10^

Figure 2 sorts the people in this market into two groups. Those who must make payments abroad, such as importers, need dollars. Together they make up the **demand for foreign exchange**. On the other side, exporters like Selvi, whose garment unit in Tiruppur sells T-shirts to American shops, and workers abroad like Ravi, who send home **remittances**, bring dollars in. They make up the **supply of foreign exchange**.^3^ Every deal is two purchases at once: when Meera’s father buys dollars, he is also selling rupees.^10^ The exchange rate is the price at which the two sides match (see Section 4).

## Most currency trading is not about trade

In April 2025, about US\$9.6 trillion worth of currencies changed hands every day.^6^ All the world’s exports of goods and services in 2024 were worth about 33 trillion dollars.^11^ So the forex market trades a whole year of world exports in less than four days. Most of this trading is done by banks and investors moving savings between countries.

Investors come in two kinds. **Foreign direct investment (FDI)**, like a carmaker building a factory near Chennai, stays for years. **Foreign portfolio investment (FPI)** buys shares and bonds, and this investment can leave in a single day.^10^ That is why its comings and goings can jolt the rupee (see Section 9).

::: {custom-style="Key Term"}
**Foreign direct investment (FDI)**  Buying a lasting stake (10% or more) in a business abroad, or setting one up.
:::

::: {custom-style="Key Term"}
**Foreign portfolio investment (FPI)**  Buying shares and bonds (holdings under 10%), which can be sold quickly.
:::

To buy dollars for the fees, Meera’s father will simply walk into his bank in Chennai. Under the Foreign Exchange Management Act, 1999, only banks and firms licensed by the RBI, such as **authorised dealers** and money changers, may deal in foreign currency. They trade with customers, with each other and with banks abroad, while the RBI makes the rules and, at times, buys or sells dollars itself (see Section 7).

# 3 The rupee’s account book: the balance of payments

Every dollar that enters or leaves India is written down in the **balance of payments (BoP)**, the record of all economic transactions between the residents of a country and the rest of the world over a period such as a year. The **current account** records trade in goods and services, along with income and transfers such as remittances. The **capital account** records the buying and selling of assets, such as FDI, FPI and foreign loans. When a country spends more foreign currency on its current account than it earns, it runs a **current account deficit (CAD)**. The gap must then be paid for by a net inflow on the capital account or by drawing down the country’s reserves.^3^

India imports far more goods than it exports, mainly oil, gold and electronics. But India also sells services such as software abroad, and Indians working abroad send money home. The dollars from these two sources cover most of the gap. In 2025-26 these and other **invisibles** (services, income and transfers) brought in a net US\$312 billion, leaving a current account deficit of only US\$25.2 billion, about 0.6 per cent of GDP.^15^ The capital account could not fill even this gap, because foreign portfolio investors took out a net US\$16.4 billion. So the RBI drew about US\$23.6 billion from its reserves (see Section 7).

# 4 How the exchange rate is determined: demand and supply

## Finding the equilibrium exchange rate

In Section 2, we saw that people and businesses demand US dollars to make payments abroad, while dollars enter the Indian foreign exchange market through sources such as export earnings and remittances. How do these demands and supplies determine the exchange rate?

We can answer this question using a demand-and-supply model. The demand curve shows the quantity of dollars that buyers wish to purchase at different exchange rates. The supply curve shows the quantity of dollars that sellers wish to supply at those rates.

The demand curve slopes downward. When a dollar costs fewer rupees, foreign goods, foreign education and travel abroad become cheaper for Indians, so they wish to buy more dollars. The supply curve slopes upward. When each dollar fetches more rupees, selling abroad becomes more rewarding for exporters and sending money home becomes more attractive for workers abroad, so more dollars are offered.

The two curves meet at a point called the **equilibrium exchange rate**. At this exchange rate, the quantity of dollars demanded equals the quantity supplied. There is no excess demand or excess supply at the intersection, so the model identifies no pressure for the exchange rate to change from that point.

Away from the equilibrium, there is such pressure. If the rate is above it, sellers offer more dollars than buyers want. This **excess supply** pushes the rate down. If the rate is below it, buyers want more dollars than are on offer. This **excess demand** pushes the rate up. Either way, the rate moves back towards the equilibrium.

To represent this in a diagram, put the exchange rate, measured as rupees per US dollar (₹/\$), on the vertical axis and the quantity of dollars traded on the horizontal axis. The downward-sloping demand curve and upward-sloping supply curve intersect at the equilibrium exchange rate (Figure 4a). The position of this intersection depends on both curves, not on either curve alone.

## What happens when demand or supply changes?

The equilibrium exchange rate need not remain at the same level. When the demand for dollars or their supply changes, the curves shift, and the market moves towards a new equilibrium.

**An increase in the demand for dollars.** Suppose more Indians begin travelling to the United States or purchasing foreign goods and services. At each exchange rate, buyers now wish to purchase more dollars than before, so the demand curve shifts to the right. In panel (b) of Figure 4, the equilibrium moves from ₹82 to ₹86 per US dollar. Buyers must now pay more rupees for each dollar, which means that the rupee has depreciated against the dollar.

**An increase in the supply of dollars.** Now suppose Indian exporters receive more dollar payments from customers abroad. At each exchange rate, sellers are willing to supply more dollars than before, so the supply curve shifts to the right. In panel (c), the exchange rate falls from ₹82 to ₹79. Each dollar can now be exchanged for fewer rupees, so the rupee has appreciated against the dollar.

## Using the model to understand exchange-rate movements

These examples isolate one change at a time. In the first, demand increases while supply is held constant. In the second, supply increases while demand is held constant.

Notice the difference between a movement along a curve and a shift of the curve. A change in the exchange rate itself moves buyers and sellers along their curves. A change in anything else, such as travel plans or export orders, shifts a whole curve.

Actual foreign exchange markets are more complicated. Demand and supply may shift together. For example, stronger demand for imports may increase the demand for dollars, while higher export earnings increase their supply. The resulting exchange rate depends on the combined effects of these changes. As the demand-and-supply model shows, we must consider both curves to understand where the new equilibrium will lie (see Section 9).

# 5 Appreciation and depreciation: who wins, who loses?

A change in the exchange rate does not affect everyone in the same way. The result depends on whether a person or business needs to buy foreign currency to make payments or receives foreign currency as income.

## When the rupee depreciates

Suppose the exchange rate rises from ₹82 to ₹86 per US dollar. The rupee has **depreciated** because each US dollar now costs more rupees. This changes the cost of international transactions.

An Indian importer who pays a supplier in dollars must spend more rupees for the same dollar payment. The same applies to Meera, who is preparing to study in the United States: if her college fees are fixed in dollars, their rupee cost rises when the rupee depreciates.

For an Indian exporter, however, depreciation may be advantageous. Selvi runs a garment business in Tiruppur and receives payments from foreign buyers in dollars. When those dollars are converted into rupees, each dollar brings in more rupees. Indian goods may also become cheaper for foreign buyers when their prices are expressed in dollars, improving exporters’ price competitiveness. The size of this advantage depends on how buyers respond to price changes.

Meera’s uncle Ravi, who works in Dubai and sends money to India, illustrates another effect. If the remittance is sent in a foreign currency and its value against the rupee rises, the same amount of foreign currency converts into more rupees for the recipient.

## When the rupee appreciates

If the exchange rate falls from ₹86 to ₹82 per US dollar, the rupee **appreciates**. The effects are broadly reversed. Importers and students paying dollar-denominated expenses need fewer rupees to buy the same number of dollars. Meera’s fees, for example, become less expensive in rupee terms.

Selvi may face the opposite effect: her dollar earnings convert into fewer rupees, and her garments may become more expensive for foreign buyers if their rupee prices remain unchanged. Ravi’s family may likewise receive fewer rupees for the same foreign-currency remittance.

Depreciation and appreciation describe a currency whose price is set by the market. When a government or central bank fixes the rate itself, a deliberate cut in the currency’s official value is called a **devaluation**, and a deliberate increase is a **revaluation**. India devalued the rupee in 1966 and again in 1991 (see Section 6).

## Why the effects differ

Table 1 summarises the main effects.

A stronger rupee can reduce the cost of foreign purchases while lowering the rupee value of foreign-currency earnings. The effect of an exchange-rate movement therefore depends on a person’s or business’s position in international transactions.

# 6 Exchange rate systems: fixed, flexible and managed

We have learnt what exchange rates are, how they affect our lives, and what makes the rupee appreciate or depreciate. But does the market always set the rate, and what systems has India used in its history? To answer this we first need to understand the different types of exchange rate systems.

## Types of exchange rate systems

An **exchange rate system** is the set of rules a country follows to manage the value of its currency against other currencies. There are three main types:

1. **Fixed exchange rate system.** In a **fixed (pegged) exchange rate** system, the central bank holds the rate at a set value against another currency, a basket of currencies or gold. It buys or sells foreign currency to keep the rate steady.
2. **Floating exchange rate system.** In a **floating (flexible) exchange rate** system, the rate is set by demand and supply in the market, with little government control. This is the market described in Section 4.
3. **Managed floating (dirty float) system.** A **managed float** is a mix of the two. The rate is mainly set by the market, with the central bank stepping in to prevent sharp swings.

Each system has its supporters, and each comes with risks, as the box below shows.

**1947–1971: Bretton Woods and the sterling peg.** After Independence in 1947, India joined the Bretton Woods system, under which the US dollar was pegged to gold at \$35 per ounce and all other currencies were pegged to the dollar. India chose to use the British pound sterling as its intervention currency rather than the dollar, largely because of its long-standing economic and colonial ties with Britain. Because the rupee moved with sterling, it was worth about ₹3.31 per dollar at first and ₹4.76 after Britain devalued the pound in 1949. In June 1966, after a war and two droughts had drained its foreign exchange, India devalued the rupee to ₹7.50 per dollar.

**1971–1975: The Nixon shock.** In 1971, the US government ended its commitment to convert dollars into gold, an event known as the Nixon shock, which caused the Bretton Woods system to collapse. India briefly shifted its peg to the US dollar in August 1971, but returned to sterling in December of the same year. This was beneficial because sterling’s depreciation allowed the rupee to depreciate with it, improving India’s export competitiveness during the oil crisis of 1973.

**1975–1991: A basket of currencies.** When sterling itself became unstable in the mid-1970s, India moved away from pegging to a single currency. In 1975, the RBI linked the rupee to a weighted basket of its major trading partners’ currencies, whose exact make-up the RBI did not disclose. This made the rupee more stable, since weakness in one currency could be offset by strength in others. However, by the late 1980s, India was spending far more on imports than it earned from exports and borrowing heavily to cover the gap, setting the stage for crisis.

**1991: The balance of payments crisis.** By 1991, India’s foreign exchange reserves had fallen so low that the country could pay for only about two weeks of imports. To raise emergency funds, the RBI pledged about 47 tonnes of gold with the Bank of England and the Bank of Japan, after 20 tonnes had already been sold abroad with an option to buy them back.^17^ To restore export competitiveness, the rupee was devalued by approximately 18–20 per cent against the dollar over just three days (1–3 July 1991), and a broader programme of economic liberalisation was launched.

**1992–1993: The dual rate system (LERMS).** As a bridge to a fully market-determined system, India introduced the Liberalised Exchange Rate Management System (LERMS) in March 1992. Exporters were required to surrender 40 per cent of their foreign exchange earnings to the RBI at a fixed official rate, while the remaining 60 per cent could be converted at the market rate. This arrangement lasted only until March 1993, when the two rates were unified into a single market-determined rate.
:::

## India’s exchange rate system today

Since March 1993, India has operated under a market-determined exchange rate, known as a managed float. The dual exchange rate system was abolished, and the value of the rupee became primarily set by the forces of supply and demand.

Under this system, the RBI does not fix the rupee’s value, but it does intervene from time to time by buying or selling foreign exchange to prevent excessive or abrupt fluctuations. In 1994, India also made the rupee fully convertible on the current account, meaning individuals and businesses could freely exchange rupees for foreign currencies for trade in goods, services and income, without needing prior government approval. However, full convertibility on the capital account (for investments and borrowings) is still not in place, and the RBI carefully regulates those flows to maintain financial stability.

# 7 The RBI and India’s foreign exchange reserves

We have seen how the RBI intervenes in the foreign exchange market to stabilise the rupee. But what exactly does it intervene with? The answer lies in India’s **foreign exchange reserves**, a stockpile of assets that the RBI holds and manages on behalf of the country.

India’s foreign exchange reserves are made up of four components. The largest part is **foreign currency assets**, holdings of major currencies like the US dollar, euro and pound sterling, typically invested in safe foreign government securities. The second component is **gold**. The third is **Special Drawing Rights (SDR)**, an international reserve asset created by the IMF that member countries can use to settle payments with other nations. The fourth is India’s **reserve position with the IMF**, the amount India can draw on demand without conditions.

Why does a country hold these reserves? There are three main reasons. First, they act as a buffer against economic shocks: if exports collapse or a financial panic hits, reserves can cushion the blow. Second, they ensure the country can pay for its imports even during difficult times. A commonly used measure is **import cover**, which tells us how many months of imports a country could pay for using only its reserves. Third, large reserves signal financial stability to foreign investors, encouraging investment.

Now let us look at how the RBI uses these reserves to manage the rupee’s value. Suppose the rupee is falling too fast against the US dollar, perhaps because too many people are selling rupees to buy dollars. The RBI steps in by selling dollars from its reserves into the foreign exchange market. This **intervention** increases the supply of dollars, making the dollar relatively cheaper and the rupee relatively more valuable, slowing or reversing the rupee’s fall (Figure 7). The opposite happens when the rupee is rising too fast: the RBI buys dollars, increasing demand for the dollar and gently pushing the rupee’s value back down.

:::

However, this tool has clear limits. Foreign exchange reserves, however large, are finite. If strong and sustained market pressure is pushing the rupee in one direction, the RBI cannot fight it indefinitely. This is precisely what happened in 1991, and it is a reminder that while reserves give the RBI significant power to stabilise the currency in the short run, they cannot override fundamental economic forces over the long run (see Section 11).

# 8 Nominal and real exchange rates, and purchasing power parity

So far, we have talked about the exchange rate as a single number: how many rupees it takes to buy one US dollar. This is called the **nominal exchange rate**, and it is what you see on a currency exchange board or a news ticker. However, this number alone does not tell the full story of a currency’s strength.

Consider this: suppose the nominal exchange rate stays exactly the same, say ₹83 per US dollar, for an entire year. At first glance, nothing has changed. But if prices in India rise by 10 per cent over that year while prices in the US stay flat, Indian goods have become 10 per cent more expensive for American buyers, even though the exchange rate did not move. India has effectively become less competitive as an exporter, not because of any change in the exchange rate, but because of inflation at home. This is what the **real exchange rate** captures. It adjusts the nominal rate for differences in price levels between two countries. If Indian prices are rising faster than American prices, the real exchange rate is appreciating even when the nominal rate is unchanged, making Indian exports relatively more expensive.

::: {custom-style="Box Navy Heading"}
**Worked example** How inflation changes the real exchange rate
:::

::: {custom-style="Box Navy"}
Suppose India and the US start the year with an exchange rate of ₹80 per US dollar. India experiences 8 per cent inflation while the US experiences 2 per cent inflation, and the nominal rate stays unchanged.

At the start of the year, a product priced at ₹800 in India costs \$10 in the US. By the end of the year, the same product costs ₹864 in India (up 8 per cent) but only \$10.20 in the US (up 2 per cent). At ₹80 per dollar, ₹864 converts to \$10.80, which is now more expensive than the \$10.20 US price (Figure 8). Indian exports have become less competitive even though the nominal exchange rate did not change at all. This is what it means for the real exchange rate to appreciate.
:::

![](figures/real_rate.png){width=100%}

::: {custom-style="Caption Label"}
**Figure 8.** The same product, priced in dollars, before and after a year of inflation.
:::

::: {custom-style="Caption Note"}
The exchange rate stays at ₹80 per US dollar all year. Because prices rise faster in India, the Indian product overtakes its American rival in dollar terms.
:::

This idea connects directly to a concept called **purchasing power parity (PPP)**. PPP is based on a simple idea: a basket of goods should cost the same in every country, once prices are converted into the same currency. If a basket of groceries costs ₹500 in India and \$6 in the US, then the PPP exchange rate would be roughly ₹83 per US dollar. If the actual exchange rate is very different from this, it suggests one currency is overvalued or undervalued.

However, PPP does not hold exactly in the real world. First, transport costs and import taxes mean that identical goods are not equally priced across borders. Second, many goods and services simply cannot be traded across countries at all. A haircut, a local bus ride, or a meal at a street stall in India costs far less than the equivalent in the US, not because of currency misalignment, but because these services are priced according to local wages and cannot be exported to equalise prices.

Despite its limitations, PPP is extremely useful for comparing living standards across countries. If you use the nominal exchange rate to compare incomes, a country with low prices appears poorer than it actually is. PPP adjustments correct for this by accounting for what money can actually buy in each country, giving a fairer picture of real living standards.

::: {custom-style="Box Teal Heading"}
**Did you know?** The Maharaja Mac index
:::

::: {custom-style="Box Teal"}
Every year, *The Economist* magazine publishes the Big Mac index, which compares the price of a McDonald’s burger across countries to estimate whether currencies are overvalued or undervalued. Since beef is not widely eaten in India, McDonald’s India sells a Maharaja Mac instead, and *The Economist* uses its chicken version as India’s entry in the index.

In January 2023, a Maharaja Mac cost ₹207 in India and a Big Mac cost \$5.36 in the US. This implies a PPP exchange rate of about ₹38.6 per US dollar, while the actual rate was around ₹81.7. This suggests the rupee was undervalued by about 53 per cent against the dollar, meaning money goes much further in India than the nominal exchange rate would suggest.^20^
:::

::: {custom-style="Box Teal Heading"}
**Did you know?** PPP and India’s income
:::

::: {custom-style="Box Teal"}
India’s nominal gross national income (GNI) per person, converted at market exchange rates, was about \$2,760 in 2025. Adjusted for PPP, it was about \$11,600, roughly 4.2 times higher.^21^ This gap exists because everyday goods and services in India are far cheaper than in high-income countries, so the same amount of money buys much more. International organisations like the World Bank and IMF prefer PPP figures when comparing living standards across countries for exactly this reason.
:::

# 9 What moves the rupee?

Newspapers report the rupee’s movements almost every day: ‘Rupee falls 30 paise against the dollar’. Behind each such headline sits the model from Section 4. Whenever the rupee moves, ask one question: did something change the demand for dollars, the supply of dollars, or both?

Figure 9 traces the rupee since 1991. The line climbs from about ₹18 per US dollar to about ₹95, a level first crossed in 2026.^22^ Long, quiet stretches are broken by sudden jumps, and each jump has a story behind it.

:::

::: {custom-style="Caption Note"}

The slow part of the climb is mainly about inflation. Prices in India have usually risen faster than in the United States, which slowly makes Indian goods dearer for foreign buyers and American goods cheaper for Indians (see Section 8). Fewer dollars come in through exports, more are needed for imports, and the rupee drifts down year after year.

The sudden jumps are usually about money crossing borders. When foreigners buy Indian shares, bonds or businesses, dollars flow in. When they sell, dollars flow out. These movements are called **capital flows**. Portfolio money is the quickest to leave (see Section 2), which is why it matters so much for short-run swings.

What makes this money move? Very often, interest rates. Investors compare what they can earn on an American government bond with what they can earn on an Indian one. The gap between the two is the **interest rate differential**. When the US Federal Reserve raises its interest rate, the gap narrows. Foreign investors sell Indian assets and take their dollars home, and the supply of dollars shifts to the left (Figure 10b). This is why a decision taken in Washington is front-page news in Mumbai.

::: {custom-style="Key Term"}
**interest rate differential**  Funds tend to move towards the higher return, after allowing for risk.
:::

Crude oil is the other big mover. India imports about 88 per cent of the crude oil it uses.^23^ When the world price of oil rises, refiners need more dollars to buy the same number of barrels, and the demand for dollars shifts to the right (Figure 10a). A widening current account deficit (see Section 3) has a similar effect.

![](figures/docx/ds_shocks.png){width=100%}

::: {custom-style="Caption Label"}
**Figure 10.** Two shocks in the market for US dollars (hypothetical numbers).
:::

::: {custom-style="Caption Note"}
(a) 1. The market starts at E~0~, ₹85 per dollar. 2. Oil now costs more, so refiners want more dollars at every exchange rate: demand shifts right to D~1~. 3. At ₹85 there is excess demand for dollars, and buyers bid the price up. 4. The new equilibrium E~1~ is at ₹90. Each dollar now costs more rupees, which is exactly what a depreciation of the rupee means, and more dollars change hands.
:::

::: {custom-style="Caption Note"}
(b) 1. Start again at E~0~, ₹85. 2. Foreign investors move funds to the United States, so fewer dollars are offered in India at every exchange rate: supply shifts left to S~1~. 3. The new equilibrium E~1~ is at ₹89. Again each dollar costs more rupees, so the rupee has depreciated, but this time fewer dollars change hands.
:::

Markets also look ahead. If traders expect the rupee to fall, importers rush to buy dollars today and exporters hold on to the dollars they already have. Buying or selling a currency to profit from its expected future price is called **speculation**. It can turn a fear into a fact: the rupee falls partly because so many people acted on the belief that it would.

Real episodes usually mix several of these forces. In the global financial crisis of 2008 and the COVID-19 panic of 2020, investors everywhere fled to the safety of the US dollar. India caused neither crisis, yet the rupee fell both times. Table 2 sums up the forces, and the box that follows asks you to read the rupee’s history for yourself.

# 10 Why exchange rates matter for India

You have already met the winners and losers from a change in the rupee (see Section 5). Here we follow those effects through the wider economy. Whether you gain or lose depends on whether you earn dollars, spend them or owe them.

Start with those who earn dollars. When the rupee weakens, every dollar an exporter earns turns into more rupees. The exporter can keep the dollar price unchanged and enjoy a bigger income, or cut the price abroad to win more orders. Either way, Indian goods become easier to sell, and we say that their **export competitiveness** has improved. The story of Selvi’s unit shows how this works.

::: {custom-style="Key Term"}
**export competitiveness**  The ability of a country’s firms to sell goods and services abroad at prices that foreign buyers are willing to pay.
:::

::: {custom-style="Box Navy Heading"}
**Case study** A weaker rupee in Selvi’s unit
:::

::: {custom-style="Box Navy"}
Selvi’s unit in Tiruppur sells cotton T-shirts to a buyer in the United States, priced in dollars. Last year she shipped an order worth \$20,000 when the rupee was at ₹90 per dollar, and it brought in ₹18 lakh. This year the same order, at ₹95, brings in ₹19 lakh. The extra ₹1 lakh can pay for overtime wages, or let her offer a small discount to win the next order.
:::

Now consider those who spend dollars. India imports crude oil, gold, electronics, fertiliser and edible oil, almost all priced in dollars. A weaker rupee makes each of them dearer in rupees, even when the dollar price has not changed. Diesel runs the trucks that carry vegetables, grain and medicines, so costlier fuel spreads into the price of almost everything (Figure 11). A rise in prices that comes from dearer imports is called **imported inflation**.

![](figures/docx/imported_inflation.png){width=100%}

::: {custom-style="Caption Label"}
**Figure 11.** How a weaker rupee becomes imported inflation.
:::

::: {custom-style="Caption Note"}
Read the chain from left to right. Each step takes time, so the full effect on prices can take several months to appear. The exchange rates shown are hypothetical.
:::

::: {custom-style="Box Navy Heading"}
**Worked example** The same barrel, a different price
:::

::: {custom-style="Box Navy"}
Suppose a barrel of crude oil costs \$80 on the world market (hypothetical figures).

• At ₹85 per US dollar, India pays 80 × 85 = ₹6,800 per barrel.

• At ₹90 per US dollar, India pays 80 × 90 = ₹7,200 per barrel.

The oil has not become any dearer in dollars, yet India pays ₹400 more for every barrel, a rise of about 5.9 per cent.
:::

Then there are those who owe dollars. A company that borrowed in dollars, such as an airline paying for new aircraft, must now find more rupees to repay the same loan. A sharp fall in the rupee can turn a healthy balance sheet into a worrying one.

All of this matters to the Reserve Bank of India. When the RBI raises its policy interest rate (the repo rate), Indian assets pay more, foreign money tends to flow in and the rupee strengthens. Imports become cheaper and inflation cools, although exporters find life harder. This route from interest rates to spending and prices, working through the exchange rate, is called the **exchange rate channel** of monetary policy.^4^ It is one reason the RBI keeps a close eye on the rupee whenever it sets interest rates.

# 11 When currencies collapse: crises in South Asia

Foreign exchange reserves work like a family’s savings for a bad year (see Section 7). But what happens when the savings run out? A country that cannot find enough dollars to pay for its imports and repay its foreign loans can no longer protect its currency. The result is a **currency crisis**: a sudden, steep fall in the value of a currency, usually together with shortages and soaring prices. Two of India’s neighbours have lived through one recently.

**Sri Lanka, 2022.**  Sri Lanka’s troubles built up over several years. Large tax cuts in 2019 reduced the government’s income. The COVID-19 pandemic then emptied the tourist hotels that had earned much of the country’s foreign currency, while big repayments on foreign loans kept falling due. The central bank held the Sri Lankan rupee at about 200 per dollar and spent its reserves to do so. By the end of March 2022, gross reserves were down to about US\$1.9 billion, and most of that could not easily be spent.^24^

Once the currency was allowed to float in March 2022, it crashed, from about 202 rupees per dollar in February to about 356 by May (Figure 12). Fuel, cooking gas and medicines ran short, and people queued for hours outside petrol pumps. In April 2022 the government stopped paying its foreign debts, Sri Lanka’s first **sovereign default**. Inflation reached 69.8 per cent in September 2022.^25^ In March 2023 the IMF approved a loan of about US\$3 billion,^26^ and prices slowly began to settle.

::: {custom-style="Caption Label"}
**Figure 12.** The Sri Lankan and Indian rupees against the US dollar, 2021 to 2024.
:::

::: {custom-style="Caption Note"}
Both lines show how many units of local currency it takes to buy one dollar, scaled so that January 2022 = 100. A rise means the currency has weakened. Once Sri Lanka stopped defending its rupee, it lost value within weeks. The Indian rupee also weakened over 2022, but gradually.

**Pakistan, 2022–23.**  Pakistan came close to the same edge. High oil prices widened its current account deficit, floods in the summer of 2022 destroyed crops and homes, and talks with the IMF stalled. In early February 2023, the State Bank of Pakistan’s reserves fell to about US\$2.9 billion, their lowest level in nine years and barely enough for a few weeks of imports.^28^ The government restricted imports and the Pakistani rupee slid, reaching a record low of about 307 per dollar in September 2023.^29^ Inflation hit 38 per cent in May 2023.^30^ A US\$3 billion **IMF programme**, approved in July 2023, helped Pakistan avoid default.^31^

::: {custom-style="Key Term"}
**IMF programme**  A loan from the International Monetary Fund to a country short of foreign currency, paid in instalments on condition that the country carries out agreed policy changes.
:::

**What about India?**  Both stories echo India’s own crisis of 1991 (see Section 6), when reserves covered only about two weeks of imports. India’s position today is much stronger. Its reserves stood at about US\$682 billion in May 2026, enough for about 11 months of imports.^32^ Under a managed float, the rupee adjusts a little every day, so pressure is released gradually instead of building up behind a fixed rate. And earnings from software services and remittances cover a large part of the gap in goods trade (see Section 3).

The lesson from all three cases is that currencies rarely collapse because of a single bad event. Low reserves, heavy foreign-currency debt and a large current account deficit are like dry wood. A pandemic, an oil shock or a flood only supplies the spark.

::: {custom-style="Box Orange Heading"}
**When economists disagree** Is the IMF’s medicine too bitter?
:::

::: {custom-style="Box Orange"}
IMF loans come with conditions. Under its 2023 programme, for example, Sri Lanka agreed to raise taxes and to price fuel and electricity so that they cover their costs.

Supporters argue that the conditions fix the problems that caused the crisis in the first place. They also reassure other lenders and investors, who return only when they trust the government’s plans. Without reform, they say, the loan would simply be spent and the crisis would return.

Critics reply that raising taxes and cutting spending in the middle of a crisis makes the slump deeper. The burden falls hardest on poor families, who spend most of their income on food and fuel. And conditions drawn up in Washington may not suit local circumstances.

What do you think? Should help in a crisis come with strings attached?
:::

# Conclusion

Now look again at Meera’s fees. In 2026, dearer oil raised India’s demand for dollars, while foreign investors selling Indian assets cut the supply (see Section 9). The RBI sold dollars from its reserves to slow the rupee’s fall but did not stop it, and the bill for her fees went up by ₹2.5 lakh. The same move gave Ravi’s family more rupees for every dirham. An exchange rate is just a price, but so much of India’s trade, savings and borrowing passes through it that this one price reaches almost every household in the country.

::: {custom-style="Box Plum Heading"}
**Key points** What this chapter has shown
:::

::: {custom-style="Box Plum"}
• **Why a currency has a price.** When goods, services or savings cross a border, one currency must be swapped for another. The exchange rate is the price of that swap (Section 1).

• **Who buys and sells currencies.** Importers, students abroad and Indians investing abroad demand dollars. Exporters, workers abroad, foreign tourists and foreign investors supply them, through banks regulated by the RBI (Sections 2 and 3).

• **What sets the price, and why it keeps changing.** The demand for and supply of dollars. Inflation, interest rates, capital flows, oil prices and expectations keep shifting the two curves (Sections 4, 8 and 9).

• **How it is managed.** Countries choose fixed, floating or managed rates. Since 1993 India has used a managed float, with the RBI using its reserves to smooth sharp swings, though no country’s reserves can hold a rate for ever (Sections 6, 7 and 11).

• **Who gains and who loses.** A weaker rupee helps exporters and families receiving remittances, but hurts importers, students abroad and anyone with dollar debts, and it can push up prices at home (Sections 5 and 10).

**Where next?** The balance of payments, monetary policy and international trade each build on the ideas in this chapter.
:::

# Glossary

**export competitiveness**  The ability of a country’s firms to sell goods and services abroad at prices that foreign buyers are willing to pay.

**foreign direct investment (FDI)**  Buying a lasting stake (10% or more) in a business abroad, or setting one up.

**foreign portfolio investment (FPI)**  Buying shares and bonds (holdings under 10%), which can be sold quickly.

**IMF programme**  A loan from the International Monetary Fund to a country short of foreign currency, paid in instalments on condition that the country carries out agreed policy changes.

# Review questions

### Sections 1–3: Exchange rates, the forex market and the balance of payments

1. The rate moves from ₹90 to ₹95 per US dollar. Has the rupee become stronger or weaker? Explain.

    *Answer.* Weaker. Each dollar now costs more rupees, so each rupee buys fewer dollars.

2. Write the direct quote “US\$1 = ₹80” as an indirect quote.

    *Answer.* ₹1 = US\$0.0125 (1 ÷ 80).

3. A money changer buys dollars at ₹94 and sells them at ₹96. What is the spread on US\$100?

    *Answer.* ₹200 (₹9,600 − ₹9,400).

4. Give two sources of demand for dollars and two sources of supply of dollars in India.

    *Answer.* Demand: importers of oil or gold, students paying foreign fees, Indian tourists abroad, Indians investing abroad. Supply: exporters, remittances, foreign tourists in India, FDI and FPI inflows.

5. Why does a fall in the rupee against the dollar mean more rupees for Ravi, even though he is paid in dirhams?

    *Answer.* The dirham is fixed at 3.6725 per US dollar. When the dollar costs more rupees, so does the dirham.

6. What is the difference between FDI and FPI, and which is more likely to cause sudden moves in the rupee?

    *Answer.* FDI buys a lasting stake in a business and stays for years. FPI buys shares and bonds and can leave in a day. FPI is more likely to cause sudden moves.

7. Why might Selvi sign a forward contract?

    *Answer.* To fix today the rate at which she will sell her dollars later, so that a stronger rupee does not wipe out her profit.

8. India imports far more goods than it exports. Why is its current account deficit still small?

    *Answer.* Large net earnings from invisibles, mainly software exports and remittances, cover most of the trade gap.

9. How can a country pay for a current account deficit?

    *Answer.* With a net inflow on the capital account (such as FDI, FPI or loans) or by drawing down its foreign exchange reserves.

### Sections 4–8: How the rate is set and managed

1. At ₹80 per US dollar, buyers want US\$120 million and sellers offer US\$100 million. Is there excess demand or excess supply, and which way will the rate move?

    *Answer.* Excess demand of US\$20 million. Buyers bid the rate up, so the rupee depreciates until the two quantities are equal.

2. What is the difference between a depreciation and a devaluation? Give one Indian example of a devaluation.

    *Answer.* A depreciation is a fall in a market-set rate, while a devaluation is a deliberate cut in an official, fixed rate. India devalued the rupee in June 1966 (to ₹7.50 per dollar) and in July 1991.

3. Name the three main types of exchange rate system. Which one has India used since March 1993?

    *Answer.* Fixed (pegged), floating (flexible) and managed float. India has used a managed float since 1993.

4. Draw a demand-and-supply diagram to show how the RBI can slow a fall in the rupee. What happens to India’s reserves?

    *Answer.* Supply of dollars shifts right as the RBI sells dollars, so the rate rises by less than it otherwise would (as in Figure 7). Reserves fall by the dollars sold.

5. A pair of shoes costs ₹2,400 in India and \$40 in the US. What PPP exchange rate does this imply? If the market rate is ₹95 per dollar, is the rupee overvalued or undervalued by this measure?

    *Answer.* ₹60 per dollar (2,400 ÷ 40). At ₹95 a dollar buys more in India than PPP suggests, so the rupee is undervalued.

### Sections 9–11: What moves the rupee, why it matters, and crises

1. The US Federal Reserve raises its interest rate sharply. Assuming nothing else changes, what is most likely to happen in the market for US dollars in India? (a) The demand curve for dollars shifts left and the rupee appreciates. (b) The supply curve of dollars shifts left and the rupee depreciates. (c) The supply curve of dollars shifts right and the rupee appreciates. (d) Nothing changes, because the RBI has not changed its own interest rate.

    *Answer.* (b). The interest rate differential narrows, so foreign investors move funds out of India. Fewer dollars are offered at every exchange rate, and the ₹/\$ rate rises.

2. Which of the following is an example of imported inflation? (a) Vegetable prices rise after a weak monsoon. (b) Diesel prices rise after the rupee falls from ₹85 to ₹90 per US dollar. (c) House rents rise in Bengaluru as more IT firms open offices. (d) Wages rise after a new minimum wage law.

    *Answer.* (b). Only (b) starts with imports becoming dearer in rupees. The others are caused by domestic supply or demand conditions.

3. In 2022, world crude oil prices rose and the US Federal Reserve raised interest rates at the same time. (i) Draw a diagram of the market for US dollars in India showing both changes. (ii) Explain why we can be sure what happened to the value of the rupee, but not to the quantity of dollars traded.

    *Answer.* (i) Demand for dollars shifts right (refiners need more dollars for oil), and supply shifts left (foreign investors take funds to the US). (ii) Both shifts push the ₹/\$ rate up, so the rupee clearly depreciates. The demand shift raises the quantity traded while the supply shift lowers it, so the net effect on quantity depends on which shift is larger.

4. Which combination of conditions left Sri Lanka most exposed to a currency crisis in 2022? (a) High reserves, little foreign debt and a booming tourism sector. (b) Low reserves, heavy foreign debt repayments and a collapse in tourism earnings. (c) A flexible exchange rate and fast-growing exports. (d) Rising remittances and falling world oil prices.

    *Answer.* (b). Low reserves, heavy foreign-currency debt and a sudden loss of foreign earnings are the ‘dry wood’ described in Section 11.
